# 2. Bonds

Bonds have the following properties:
- Price
- Coupon Rate
- Face Value Maturity

The idea is that the investor receives a coupon every year, then a face value when the bond matures. An example cash flow is:


| Time (Years) | Cash Flow ($) |
| ------------ | ------------- |
| 0            | -98           |
| 1            | 6             |
| 2            | 6             |
| 3            | 6             |
| 4            | 6             |
| 5            | 106           |


In this case, the 
- Price is $98
- The coupon rate is 6%, and the annual coupon payment is $6
- The face value is $100


The formula for a bond’s coupon rate is: (Sum of annual coupon payments / \[Face] value) x 100[^1]; therefore, in this case, coupon rate is $\$6 / \$100 = 6\%$.

In the final year, the $100 face value pays with the coupon, hence the $106.

The reason we say "Sum of annual coupon payments" is because some bonds pay **semi-annually** and pay, for instance, twice per year, every 6 months. You would sum these two smaller payments to get the total annual payment for the coupon rate.

$$\text{Coupon Rate} = \frac{\sum_{\text{year}}\text{Coupon Payments}}{\text{Face Value}}$$

## Types of Bonds

**Government Bonds** (aka Treasury Bonds) have the following categories:
- *T-bills* last up to a year
- *T-notes* last up to 3 years
- *T-bonds* last up to 30 years

There are also **Corporate Bonds.**

Treasury bonds, in general, are considered to be safer than corporate bonds. As a consequence, treasury bonds tend to offer less return than corporate bonds, because they are safer.

## Zero-Coupon Bond

A zero coupon bond (also called a **strip**), pays no coupons; you just get the face value later.

The **yield** of a coupon is the annual rate that corresponds to a bond. Basically the ROI (I think).

For a zero-coupon bond, you simply use the [TVM equation](1.%20Time%20Value%20of%20Money.md#^tvm).

---

*Example:* A 3-year zero coupon bond has a price of \$98 and a Face Value of \$100. What is the yield? 

Using the TVM equation gives us:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{FV} &= {\rm PV}\left( 1 + \frac{i}{n} \right)^{n\cdot t}\\
100 &= 98 \left( 1+i \right)^{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

Solving for $i$ gives:

$$i=\left(\frac{100}{98}\right)^{1/3}-1=0.675\%$$

This shows the relationship between yield and time.

To price a bond, that is to find the $\rm PV$ of it, based on a yield curve like this, you need to apply the rate to each year individually.

To do this, at each bond payment, apply the [TVM equation](1.%20Time%20Value%20of%20Money.md#^tvm) with an $i$ that corresponds to the yield curve at $t$ years.

$$i(t) = \text{Yield Curve}(t)$$

Then, sum the present values to get the total present value. An example of this in excel is:

![[image-142.png]]

The `Sum` column is the sum of `D`.

This sum is how much the company sells the bond for; thus, if they need to raise some amount of capital $C$, then the number of bonds they need to sell $N$ is:

$$N = \frac{C}{\text{PV}_{\text{of one bond}}}$$

The **Yield Spread** or **Yield Curve Spread** of a company's yield curve $\rm YC$ is simply:

$$\text{Spread}(t) = \text{YC}_\text{Company}(t)-\text{YC}_\text{Treasury}(t)$$
This is a function of time $t$. The corporate $\rm YC$ at any $t$ is almost always higher than the treasury due to the increased risk.

### Yield Curve Arbitrage 

In some instances, the yield curve is monotonically increasing:
$$\frac{d\, \text{YC}}{dt} \ge 0 \quad \forall t \ge 0$$

However, in some cases, $d\ {\rm YC} / dt < 0$; this is called *inversion.*

![](https://actuaries.blog.gov.uk/wp-content/uploads/sites/232/2020/06/inverted-curves-1-updated-1000x563.jpg) 
An inverted yield curve is a sign of an economic recession. Almost every recession has been preceded by an inverted yield curve. An exception is COVID, which was a pure shock.

This lets people take advantage of the curve by:
1. Borrowing money 2 years out at a low rate
2. Lending that borrowed money at 1 year at a high rate
3. The interest you make exceeds what you owe, you walk away with cash.

## Law of One Price

If you can replicate the cash flow of a bond using a series of zero-coupon bonds, then the $\rm PV$ of the bond is equal to the sum of the constituent bond's $\rm PV$s.

Each row (minus the last which is a sum) is a multiple of some other bond offered. The multiple is how many we are buying—shown in the far right column. As seen, if you can make all the columns sum to meet the desired face value and coupon, then it follows that the summed $\rm PV$ is equivalent to the present value. This is known as the **Law of One Price**.

## Amortization Schedule

> This is literally just a loan, not sure why it's in this section.

The idea is, you know your rate, the number of periods, and the present value (principle). By the end, the future value is zero after you have paid everything off.

To do this, you can use the `PMT` function in excel to find the payment, which is the same every year.

However, each payment, although constant, contributes a nonconstant ratio to interest and principle.

Each year, you have some principal $P_i$. $P_0$ is the $\rm PV$ at the start of the loan.

At each payment, the principle owed is $P_i \times i$. The remainder of the payment is:

$$\text{PMT}-P_i\times i = \text{Remainder}$$

This remainder then goes towards paying down the principle, such that the new principle $P_{i+1}$ is:

$$P_{i+1}=P_i-\text{Remainder}$$
On the $i^{th}$ cycle, the **ending principle** refers to $P_{i+1}$, after the remainder is subtracted.


## Yield to Maturity

If you have two bonds with different maturities, and you want to evaluate which is a better investment, you can simply calculate the rate/yield for each. Whichever has a higher yield is a better investment. This, however, assumes the rate is constant per year. 

If you have a semiannual bond, you need to divide the annual rate by two (assuming two compounds per year) before applying the equations.

## Different Rates

The following rates should not be confused:
- Coupon Rate
- Interest Rate /  Yield to Maturity (YTM)

We will find that, depending on the coupon rate and YTM, the face value may be more or less than the price.


| Coupon Rate vs YTM | Price vs Face Value |
| ------------------ | ------------------- |
| Coupon Rate > YTM  | Price > Face Value  |
| Coupon Rate < YTM  | Price < Face Value  |
| Coupon Rate = YTM  | Price = Face Value  |

The duration describes the slope of this line, $dp/dy$:

$$\text{Duration} = - \frac{(1+y)}{P} \cdot \frac{dP}{dy}$$

where $y$ is the yield and $P$ is the price. The modified duration is similarly:

$$\text{Modified Duration}= -\frac{1}{P}\cdot \frac{dP}{dy}$$

So both give a measure of how sensitive the price is to a change in yield.
