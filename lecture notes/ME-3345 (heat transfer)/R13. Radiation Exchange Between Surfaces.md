# R13. Radiation Exchange Between Surfaces

> This class limits the discussion to opaque diffuse gray surfaces.


The **view factor** $F_{ij}$ is "the fraction of the radiation leaving surface $i$ that is intercepted by surface $j$."
The subscripts give the direction, so $i\to j \Rightarrow F_{ij}$  and $j \to i \Rightarrow F_{ji}$. It's important to realize that $F_{ij}\ne F_{ji}$ except when their areas are equal.

![](https://docs.cemosis.fr/ibat/latest/models/_images/rht/view-factor-geometry.png)

The view factor for $i\to j$ is:

$$F_{ij} = \frac{1}{A_i} \int_{A_i}\int_{A_j}\frac{\cos \theta_i\cos\theta_j}{\pi R^2}dA_idA_j$$

And for $j\to i$:

$$F_{ji} = \frac{1}{A_j}\int_{A_j}\int_{A_i}\frac{\cos \theta_j\cos\theta_i}{\pi R^2}dA_j\,dA_i$$

It follows that:

$$A_iF_{ij} = A_jF_{ji}$$

Is known as the **reciprocity relation**. 

For surfaces in an enclosure made up of $N$ surfaces, the *summation rule* (also known as the *enclosure law*) states:

$$\sum_{j=1}^NF_{ij}=1$$
And, for concave surfaces, it is possible for a surface to "see itself" and $F_{ii}\ne 0$. 

> [!info]+ AI-generated addition (F_ii complement)

Conversely, if a surface is **plane or convex**, it cannot see itself:

$$F_{ii} = 0 \qquad \text{(plane or convex surface)}$$

This is the rule you'll actually use most for finding view factors by inspection. Combined with reciprocity, summation, and symmetry, it usually nails most enclosure problems without ever touching the double integral.

There are $N^2$ view factors for $N$ surfaces in an enclosure; this can be seen when written in matrix form:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} F_{11} & F_{12} & \cdots & F_{1N} \\ F_{21} & F_{22} & \cdots & F_{2N} \\ \vdots & & \ddots & \vdots \\ F_{N1} & F_{N2} & \cdots & F_{NN} \end{bmatrix}
$$

Each *row* can be used with the summation rule. But you rarely need to find all of these to solve a problem.

## Blackbody Radiation Exchange

Radiation exchange between two black bodies is relatively easy because they do not reflect; the only transfer is via radiation/absorption. 

The radiation leaving surface $i$ and is being *intercepted* by surface $j$ is:

$$q_{i \to j} = (A_iJ_i)F_{ij} = A_iF_{ij}E_{bi}$$

where $J_i$ is the [radiosity](R12.%20Radiation.md#^radiosity), which is just $E_{bi}$ for the black surface.

The same can be expressed for ${j\to i}$:

$$q_{j \to i} = A_jF_{ji}E_{bj}$$

The net radiative exchange is simply the difference between these two:

$$\boxed{q_{ij} = q_{i \to j} - q_{j\to i} = A_iF_{ij}\sigma(T_i^4-T_j^4)}$$
If the surface is in an enclosure of $N$ black surfaces, then:

$$q_i = \sum^N_{j=1}A_iF_{ij}\sigma(T_i^4-T_j^4)$$

where $q_i$ is the net transfer of radiation from surface $i$.

The net rate at which radiation leaves a surface $i$ is $q_i$, given as:

$$q_i = A_i(J_i-G_i)$$

Because, by definition, the [radiosity](R12.%20Radiation.md#^radiosity) is $J_i = E_i+\zeta_i G_i$, this can be written as:

$$q_i = A_i(E_i-(1-\zeta_i)G_i)$$

And, if the material is opaque, then $\alpha = 1-\zeta$ and:

$$q_i = A_i(E_i-\alpha_iG_i)$$

Because $\epsilon_i = \alpha_i$, $E_i = \alpha_i E_{bi}$ and this can be written as:

$$\begin{aligned}
q_i &= A_i(\epsilon_iE_{bi}-\epsilon_iG_i)\\
&=A_i \left( \epsilon_iE_{bi}-\epsilon_i \frac{(J_i-E_i)}{\zeta _i}\right)\\
&=A_i \epsilon_i\left( E_{bi}-\frac{(J_i-\epsilon_iE_{bi})}{1-\epsilon_i}\right)\\
&=A_i \epsilon_i\left( \frac{E_{bi}(1-\epsilon_i)}{(1-\epsilon_i)} - \frac{(J_i-\epsilon_iE_{bi})}{1-\epsilon_i}\right)\\
&=A_i \epsilon_i\left( \frac{E_{bi}(1-\epsilon_i)-(J_i-\epsilon_iE_{bi})}{1-\epsilon_i}\right)\\
&=A_i \epsilon_i\left( \frac{E_{bi}-J_i}{1-\epsilon_i}\right)\\
q_i&= \frac{E_{bi}-J_i}{\displaystyle \left(\frac{1-\epsilon_i}{\epsilon_iA_i}\right)}
\end{aligned}$$

This gives the concept of radiative resistance, between the black body emission and actual radiosity:

$$\partial R_{\rm rad} = \frac{1-\epsilon_i}{\epsilon_iA_i}$$

In a $N$ surface enclosure:

$$q_i = \frac{E_{bi}-J_i}{\displaystyle \left(\frac{1-\epsilon_i}{\epsilon_iA_i}\right)} = \sum^N_{j=1}\frac{J_i-J_j}{(A_iF_{ij})^{-1}}
$$

The $(A_iF_{ij})^{-1}$ represents the geometric resistance, which works in tandem with the prior resistance as shown below:

**Large (infinite) parallel plates** — $A_1 = A_2 = A$, $F_{12}=1$:

$$q_{12} = \frac{\sigma A(T_1^4-T_2^4)}{\displaystyle \frac{1}{\epsilon_1}+\frac{1}{\epsilon_2}-1}$$

**Long concentric cylinders** — surface 1 inner, surface 2 outer, $F_{12}=1$:

$$q_{12} = \frac{\sigma A_1(T_1^4-T_2^4)}{\displaystyle \frac{1}{\epsilon_1}+\frac{1-\epsilon_2}{\epsilon_2}\left(\frac{r_1}{r_2}\right)}$$

**Concentric spheres** — same idea but $(r_1/r_2)^2$:

$$q_{12} = \frac{\sigma A_1(T_1^4-T_2^4)}{\displaystyle \frac{1}{\epsilon_1}+\frac{1-\epsilon_2}{\epsilon_2}\left(\frac{r_1}{r_2}\right)^2}$$

**Small convex object in a large cavity** (eq. 13.32) — $A_1/A_2 \to 0$, $F_{12}=1$, the cavity behaves like a blackbody at $T_2$ regardless of $\epsilon_2$:

$$\boxed{q_{12} = \epsilon_1\sigma A_1(T_1^4-T_{sur}^4)}$$

This last one is the syllabus's named equation. Watch for any "small object in a large room/surroundings" wording.

### Reradiating Surface

> [!info]+ AI-generated addition (reradiating surface concept)
> This is what was missing in the "Not sure what to do if there are 2..." spot. Verify against lecture-25.

A **reradiating surface** is one with no net heat transfer: $q_i = 0$. Physically this means it's well-insulated on the back side, or it's reached steady state with no other heat exchange. Setting $q_i = 0$ in the surface-resistance equation gives:

$$q_i = \frac{E_{bi}-J_i}{(1-\epsilon_i)/(\epsilon_i A_i)} = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad J_i = E_{bi} = \sigma T_i^4$$

So the radiosity equals the blackbody emissive power, and $T_i$ floats to whatever value the network demands. In a 3-surface enclosure where surface 3 is reradiating, the network simplifies a lot:

- Node $J_3$ floats — no surface resistance branch matters because $q_3 = 0$
- The total resistance from $E_{b1}$ to $E_{b2}$ becomes: surface resistance 1, then the parallel combination of [direct $1{\to}2$] and [$1{\to}3{\to}2$ in series], then surface resistance 2.

$$R_{tot} = \frac{1-\epsilon_1}{\epsilon_1 A_1} + \left[\frac{1}{A_1F_{12}} \;\big\|\; \left(\frac{1}{A_1F_{13}}+\frac{1}{A_2F_{23}}\right)\right] + \frac{1-\epsilon_2}{\epsilon_2 A_2}$$

Then $q_1 = -q_2 = (E_{b1}-E_{b2})/R_{tot}$. To find $T_3$: solve for $J_3$ via the current-divider rule, then $T_3 = (J_3/\sigma)^{1/4}$.

### Radiation Shields

> [!info]+ AI-generated addition (shield formula)

A radiation shield is a thin, high-reflectivity sheet placed between two surfaces to cut radiative transfer. Each shield adds two surface resistances (one per side) plus a geometric resistance.

For $N$ shields between two surfaces where **all emissivities are equal**:

$$\boxed{(q_{12})_N = \frac{1}{N+1}(q_{12})_0}$$

where $(q_{12})_0$ is the heat transfer with no shield. So 1 shield halves the transfer, 2 shields cut to a third, etc.
